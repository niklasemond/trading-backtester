Indicator

Simple moving average (SMA)

An SMA is the average closing price over the last N bars. A 20-day SMA reacts faster to recent prices than a 200-day SMA because each new day represents a much larger share of its calculation.

In the appFast SMA and Slow SMA are the two main strategy inputs. The research tab lets you test several window lengths at once.
Trading intuitionShorter windows are more responsive but noisier. Longer windows are smoother but slower to react when a trend changes.
4 bars
Drag the window. Smaller values hug price more closely; larger values smooth more noise but react later.

SMAs are most useful here as building blocks for a crossover and a trend filter.

Signal

SMA crossover

A crossover looks for the moment a faster moving average moves from one side of a slower average to the other. In this app, “fast crosses above slow” is the basic long entry signal and “fast crosses below slow” is the basic exit signal.

A crossover reduces a continuous price series to discrete trade signals. Because the signal is only known after the bar closes, this app uses next-bar execution.

Filter

Trend filter

The trend filter asks for the closing price to be above the slow SMA before a new long position can be opened. It is a simple way of saying: “Only take bullish signals when the broader price trend is already positive.”

Literal ruleClose > slow SMA.
Trade-offIt may avoid some weak markets, but it can also make entries later and miss sharp reversals.
Indicator / filter

Momentum / rate of change (ROC)

Momentum measures how much the price has changed over a chosen lookback. In this app, ROC compares today’s price with the price N bars ago.

ROC = current price ÷ price N bars ago − 1
ExampleIf SPY moves from 400 to 440 over 126 trading days, ROC is +10%.
In the strategyA threshold of 0% means “only enter while momentum is positive.” The optional momentum exit closes the position if ROC falls below the threshold.

A momentum filter and an SMA trend rule often overlap conceptually, so identical backtest results can occur when one filter never changes the actual trade dates.

Risk / filter

Volatility

Volatility measures how widely daily returns move around their average. It does not tell you whether the market is going up or down; it tells you how uneven the ride has been.

The app annualizes trailing daily volatility. A “vol 20 < 30%” filter means the last 20 daily returns must imply annualized volatility below 30%. Volatility also appears inside the Sharpe ratio.

Execution cost

Commission

Commission is a direct fee charged when an order executes. The current model uses a fixed amount per order, so a $1 commission means $1 on a buy and another $1 on a later sell.

It matters more for strategies that trade often, especially when the portfolio is small. It is different from slippage, which changes the execution price rather than charging a separate fee.

Execution cost

Slippage and basis points (bps)

Slippage approximates the fact that you often cannot trade at the exact price printed in historical data. The engine makes buys slightly more expensive and sells slightly cheaper than the next observed open.

1 basis point (bp) = 0.01%   ·   10 bps = 0.10%   ·   100 bps = 1%
10 bps
Move the slider to see how the same market open becomes a worse buy price and a worse sell price.
Position sizing

Cash buffer and share sizing

The cash buffer is the fraction of the portfolio deliberately left uninvested. A 5% buffer means the engine tries to deploy at most about 95% of available cash when it buys.

If fractional shares are disabled, the engine rounds down to the largest whole-share position it can afford after commission. That creates a small extra cash remainder even with a 0% buffer.

Backtesting convention

Next-bar execution

The strategy calculates its signal using a day’s closing price. Since that close is not known until the bar is finished, the app does not pretend you could also execute at that same close. Instead, the order executes at the next available bar’s open.

This timing rule is one defense against look-ahead bias: accidentally letting a backtest use information that would not have existed at the moment of the trade.

Benchmark

Buy & hold benchmark

Buy-and-hold is the app’s “do nothing clever” baseline. It buys the asset at the first in-range open using the same capital, sizing, commission, and slippage assumptions, then holds through the final close.

Comparing a strategy with buy-and-hold answers a stricter question than “Did the strategy make money?” It asks, “Did the extra timing rules add value relative to simply owning the asset?” That difference is excess return.

Performance metric

Total return and CAGR

Total return is the percentage change from starting capital to final portfolio value. It answers “How much did the portfolio grow over the entire period?”

CAGR (compound annual growth rate) converts that start-to-finish growth into the constant annual rate that would compound to the same result over the elapsed calendar time.

Total returnEasy to interpret, but depends heavily on how long the backtest lasts.
CAGRBetter for comparing periods of different lengths, but still says nothing about the path or risk taken to get there.

To add information about the path, look at volatility, Sharpe ratio, and maximum drawdown.

Performance metric

Sharpe ratio

Sharpe ratio is a risk-adjusted return measure: roughly, how much average return the strategy delivered for each unit of return variability. Higher is generally more attractive when comparing strategies measured consistently.

This version uses daily returns, annualizes the result, and assumes a 0% risk-free rate. A higher Sharpe can come from stronger returns, lower volatility, or both.

Sharpe is useful, but it compresses a complicated return distribution into one number. It does not tell you the size of the worst historical loss; use drawdown for that.

Performance metric

Maximum drawdown

Drawdown measures how far the portfolio has fallen from its previous high-water mark. Maximum drawdown is the deepest peak-to-trough decline observed during the backtest.

20%
Increase the decline to see why two strategies with similar returns can feel very different to hold through.

A 20% maximum drawdown means that, at the worst point, the portfolio had fallen 20% from an earlier peak before recovering or ending. It is often more emotionally and operationally meaningful than volatility.

Research method

Training period and holdout period

The research tool splits history into two chronological parts. The training period is used to rank parameter combinations. The later holdout period is then used to see how those already-chosen rules behaved on data that did not influence their ranking.

70% / 30%
A larger training set gives more history for selecting parameters, but leaves less unseen data for validation.

If you repeatedly change parameters after looking at the holdout, it gradually stops being a true holdout. That is why the app’s robustness diagnostic is displayed after ranking rather than used to select candidates.

Research metric

Excess return vs buy-and-hold

Excess return is simply the strategy’s total return minus the buy-and-hold total return over the same period.

excess return = strategy total return − buy-and-hold total return

If a strategy returns 70% and buy-and-hold returns 60%, excess return is +10 percentage points. If the strategy returns 50%, excess return is −10 percentage points.

This app now uses training-period excess return as the default research objective because it directly asks whether the added strategy rules beat the simple baseline.

Research diagnostic

Robustness diagnostic

The robustness score is the weaker of training and holdout excess return. A positive robustness score therefore requires the strategy to beat buy-and-hold in both periods.

Positive in bothTraining and holdout excess returns are both above zero.
MixedOne period beats buy-and-hold and the other does not.

It is deliberately a diagnostic, not a ranking objective. Re-ranking after seeing the holdout would weaken the purpose of the holdout period.

Markets / asset classes

Built-in instruments: what changes when you leave SPY?

The built-in list is deliberately small and liquid, but it spans markets with different economic drivers. Testing the same rule on several instruments helps distinguish “this happened to fit SPY” from “this trading idea may describe a broader market behavior.” All eight are US-listed ETFs, while the underlying exposure can be US equities, international equities, government bonds, gold, or corporate credit.

Research principle: changing the instrument changes the market being studied. It does not make the same SMA values equally appropriate everywhere, and a strong historical result on one ETF is not evidence that the same rule will generalize.

Worked example

Putting it all together: a simple backtest

A useful backtest starts with a plain trading idea, not with random parameter hunting. Suppose your hypothesis is: “I want to own SPY when short-term trend strength is above the longer-term trend, and step aside when that relationship reverses.” That naturally maps to an SMA crossover.

1 · Choose a simple first version

InstrumentSPY. Start with something liquid and familiar so you can focus on the strategy logic rather than unusual data behavior.
Signal20-day fast SMA / 50-day slow SMA. The exact numbers are not sacred; they simply create a reasonably responsive short-vs-medium trend comparison.

For the first run, leave momentum and volatility filters off. Adding several filters immediately makes it harder to tell which rule caused an improvement or deterioration. Build complexity one piece at a time.

2 · Choose the experiment assumptions

Date rangeUse a period long enough to include different market conditions rather than only one rally or one sell-off. A decade or more is useful for a first daily-strategy check when data is available.
Capital$100,000 is convenient because percentage and dollar changes are easy to read. The strategy logic itself should not depend on choosing a round number.
CommissionIf your broker is effectively commission-free, $0 is reasonable for an initial test. Otherwise use a realistic fixed fee.
SlippageDo not assume perfect fills by default. Even something modest such as 5–10 bps can be a useful sensitivity test. See slippage.

The app will still use next-bar execution, so a signal known at today’s close executes at the next observed open rather than benefiting from information that was not yet tradable.

3 · Run the backtest and read the result in layers

First: did it make money?

Look at total return and CAGR. This tells you the basic growth outcome, but not whether the strategy added value.

Second: did it beat doing nothing?

Compare with buy-and-hold. Positive excess return means the timing rules improved total return over the baseline for that period.

Third: what did the ride look like?

Use Sharpe ratio and maximum drawdown to understand variability and worst historical loss depth.

4 · Interpret trade count and the equity curve

A strategy with only a handful of completed trades may look excellent simply because a few historical decisions happened to work well. A strategy with many trades gives you more observations, but also exposes you more heavily to commissions and slippage. Neither high nor low trade count is automatically good.

Then inspect the equity curve itself. Ask: Did the strategy outperform steadily, or did almost all the advantage come from one short period? A smooth-looking final statistic can hide long stretches of underperformance.

5 · A worked interpretation

Illustrative example only: suppose the strategy returns 140%, buy-and-hold returns 170%, Sharpe is 0.85, and maximum drawdown is 18%. The correct interpretation is not “140% is a good strategy.” It is: the strategy made money and had a particular risk profile, but it lagged the passive benchmark by 30 percentage points. If its drawdown was materially smaller than buy-and-hold, that may still be interesting—but it is a different claim from outperforming on return.

Now imagine another strategy returns 185%, buy-and-hold returns 170%, but has a 35% drawdown and much higher turnover. That strategy has positive excess return, but the extra return came with materially deeper losses and more trading friction. Whether that trade-off is attractive depends on the purpose of the strategy.

6 · Decide what to test next

Change one idea at a time. For example, keep the 20/50 crossover fixed and add a trend filter. Or keep the base strategy and add a momentum requirement. Re-run the same period and compare what actually changed: trades, return, drawdown, and excess return.

Once you start testing many parameter combinations, move from the single Backtest tab to Research and use a separate training / holdout split. The more choices you try, the easier it becomes to find a historical winner by chance.

Useful mindset: a backtest is evidence about how a rule behaved under specific historical assumptions. It is not proof that the rule will work in the future. Good research asks what changed, why it may have changed, and whether the result survives different periods and reasonable cost assumptions.

Application overview

What this app is for

Trading Backtester is a research workspace for turning explicit trading ideas into repeatable historical experiments. The current product focuses on daily, long-only single-asset strategies using US-listed securities, including equity, bond, gold, international-equity, and credit ETFs, with a declarative strategy definition that can be expanded over time.

The goal is not to produce a single “winning” strategy. It is to make assumptions visible, compare rules consistently, and separate interesting historical results from stronger evidence that a strategy may generalize.

Current scope: daily bars, one asset, long-only positioning, SMA / momentum / volatility-based rules, explicit next-bar execution, configurable costs, and buy-and-hold comparison.

How the application is organized

1 · Learn

Refresh the concepts behind indicators, execution assumptions, performance metrics, and research methodology.

2 · Backtest

Build one strategy, run it over a chosen period, and inspect its trades, equity curve, and metrics against buy-and-hold.

3 · Research

Search a bounded set of parameter combinations on training data, then inspect how the selected candidates behave on a later holdout period.

Core design choices

Explicit timing

Signals use information available at the close and execute on the next available open.

Costs are part of the experiment

Commission and slippage assumptions flow through both the strategy and benchmark.

Benchmark-first interpretation

Results show buy-and-hold alongside the strategy and emphasize excess return.

Warm-up without pre-period trading

Earlier bars can initialize indicators, but portfolio accounting begins only at the experiment start date.

Training and holdout stay separate

Research candidates are ranked on training history; holdout results are displayed as later validation evidence.

Declarative strategies

Strategies are stored as structured specifications rather than hidden browser logic, supporting reproducibility and future natural-language strategy translation.

Data and current limitations

Research-grade market data

The built-in free provider is Yahoo Finance. It is convenient for personal research, not a licensed production market-data feed.

Corporate actions

Dividend and split events are applied explicitly to held positions for both strategy and benchmark. Raw OHLC remains the accounting price series; Yahoo event data is still research-grade and may contain provider anomalies.

Not a prediction engine

Historical performance does not establish future performance. A single holdout period reduces one form of overfitting but does not eliminate selection bias or regime risk.

Still intentionally narrow

No short selling, leverage model, intraday bars, portfolio-of-assets engine, taxes, borrow costs, or advanced order types are included yet.

Personal research first

The current app is optimized for transparent experimentation rather than multi-user production features.

Definitions live in Learn

Detailed explanations of indicators, execution concepts, metrics, and research terminology now live exclusively in the Learn section.
